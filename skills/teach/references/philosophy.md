# Why this teaching works

Read this before you plan a lesson. The process in `SKILL.md` is the when. This is the how, and why the how is this shape.

Two people can give the same answers and still not know the same thing. One has a pile of disconnected facts. The other has a few core truths those facts follow from, so the connections are obvious. That connection is understanding.

Connected knowledge beats disconnected knowledge. A dependency graph beats lonely nodes. Understanding beats memorizing.

Understood facts stay put because their neighbors hold them. They also compress: the same information, fewer moving parts. Memorized facts rot the moment the cue is gone.

The felt goal is the click. A pile of lonely facts collapses into a few generating ideas. When teaching lands, that is what it feels like from the inside. Aim for it.

A mechanism you should take seriously: the brain will not fully commit to a fact it is not sure is safe. If something more fundamental might later contradict it, committing is expensive to unwind, so the brain hedges and the fact never really lands. Both principles below remove that risk, in different ways.

## Principle i. Unconditional truths first

Start from the ground. Lock in the core, always-true, caveat-free facts before anything built on top of them.

Not because bottom-up is the "correct" logical order. Because unconditional truths are the easiest thing for a brain to accept. They are safe, so they commit, and they give the first solid ground to stand on. Especially when the subject is new and there is little to connect to yet.

Keep these two words distinct. Do not overuse "axiom."

An *unconditional truth* is a fact the learner can accept as-is, at face value, with no caveats. That is a property of *how the fact is held*.

An *axiom* is a fact that follows from nothing else. A root node with no incoming edges. That is a property of *where it sits in the graph*.

They overlap. They are not synonyms. An axiom that is also caveat-free is one kind of unconditional truth. Plenty of unconditional truths *do* derive from deeper things. They simply do not need that derivation to be safely accepted. Default to "unconditional truth." Reserve "axiom" for facts that genuinely bottom out. Do not call something an axiom because it sounds foundational.

Find the few hard facts they can take at face value. Often first principles that do not depend on anything else, though they need not be true roots. There may be very few. Small and solid beats large and shaky.

They must be simple enough to accept as-is, without nuance. No "well, usually." If it needs conditions, it is not an unconditional truth yet. Dig down.

These can be committed to instantly, because nothing more fundamental will come along to contradict them. That safety is what makes them lock in. Build everything else up from these, explicitly, so each new fact is visibly resting on the foundation.

Confirm the foundation before you build. Check that each core truth actually reads as obviously, unconditionally true to *them* before you add structure on top. If a core truth does not feel rock-solid, stop and fix the foundation.

Two strong forms, when they exist. Do not force either.

Universal statements. "All X are Y" or "no X is Y." Easy to lock in because they admit no exceptions to hedge against. A clean atomic-unit version, "ALL X is done through {____}", is one strong special case. Example: "ALL communication between computers is done through sending packets." Surface it when a domain has one.

Real definitions. A genuine definition is a great place to start. Only if it is an actual definition, not a vague list of properties dressed up as one. If it is just "things that tend to be true of X," it will not anchor anything.

## Principle ii. How could I have discovered this?

Facts feel arbitrary when there is no visible reason they had to be this way. "Why does it need to be like this? Feels arbitrary." The brain will not commit to arbitrary-feeling info. The fix: make it feel discovered, not decreed.

Walk them through how they could have discovered the thing themselves. Every step must be motivated.

Start from square one. Why are we even doing this? What core problem sends us down this path?

Motivate every intermediate step too. Why try this formula? Why manipulate the equation this way? What could have led someone to this approach in the first place?

The output is turning disconnected propositions into connected ones. Adding the edges.

3Blue1Brown is the master reference for this. Nothing appears from nowhere. Every move feels like something the learner might have reached for.

### Socratic vs expository

Choose per topic and per their apparent energy.

**Socratic.** Pose the motivating problem and let them attempt the discovery before you reveal. More effortful, stronger lock-in. Default to this when they can plausibly reason their way there. "Let them attempt it" is about who speaks first, not about grading. If the question has a definite right answer, it is still a quiz, even as an open-ended prompt you then frame as multiple choice.

**Expository.** You narrate the motivated discovery path yourself, 3Blue1Brown style, no back-and-forth. Use when the topic is beyond cold-reasoning reach, or when they are low-energy and want it delivered.

When unsure, lean Socratic for things they can clearly reason about. Otherwise narrate.

## Accuracy

A wrong root or a wrong discovered step corrupts every node built on it. They have to be able to trust the teacher. One confidently delivered hallucination poisons that.

Working from memory is where this fails. The moment you are even slightly unsure of a fact, name, date, formula, definition, or claim, stop and confirm it with a researcher before you say it. Pausing to verify is always acceptable. Accuracy beats flow.

If a check changes what you were about to teach, say so plainly. Do not hide the correction.
